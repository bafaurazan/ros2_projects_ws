# The Self-Contained Rule — Zero-Knowledge Explanation

Reference for `/selfcontained`. An opt-in explanation standard: when invoked, every explanation of the researcher's own work is written **as if the reader knows literally nothing** — not "knows the project but forgot the details." Nothing. Assume the reader has never read the manuscript, has never heard the project's vocabulary, cannot open a single file while reading, and will not ask a follow-up question. If any sentence would leave that reader guessing what a word, label, claim, or number refers to, the sentence is not finished yet.

This matters because researchers marinate in their own vocabulary until it turns invisible: coined terms, section numbers, and prediction labels start functioning as *content* in their heads while remaining *noise* to everyone else — including examiners, reviewers, and the researcher themselves six months later. The rule is the antidote, applied on demand.

## How to invoke it

- `/selfcontained` before or with any explanation request — "explain §4's argument, self-contained."
- `/selfcontained <text>` — rewrite an existing passage, summary, or log entry to the standard.
- Ask for it to be held **on** for a whole session or a whole document's log entries ("keep all sweep-log entries self-contained"). It remains opt-in: it applies only when the researcher asks.

## The six requirements

1. **Define every term at first use, in plain English, in-line.** Every one — *especially* terms the researcher coined and terms used in every previous session. The researcher's own vocabulary is exactly what a cold reader cannot decode; never treat a term as safe because it is theirs.
2. **Never let a label do the work of a claim.** "§1b", "P4", "Prediction 5", "Exhibit 14", "Route B", "Finding 2" are **addresses, not content**. State what the thing actually *says*, in full, then give the address in parentheses so it can be found. Test: delete the label — if the sentence becomes meaningless, it has failed.
3. **Spell out the argument, don't gesture at it.** Not "this is self-sealing" — say *what* is claimed, *why* no possible observation could count against it, and *what follows*. Not "it collides with Appendix C" — say what Appendix C establishes, and where the two accounts give incompatible answers.
4. **Name a source's own content before leaning on it.** Not "Smith's framework predicts this" — state what Smith's framework *holds*, in a sentence, then show how that entails the prediction. The reader must be able to evaluate the inference, not just witness it.
5. **Carry the "so what" every time.** Each explanation ends with what breaks if the point is right, and what changes in the manuscript as a result.
6. **No compression, no notation shorthand, no telegraphic clauses.** Length is not a cost here; a reader stopping to guess is. Prefer the longer sentence that lands to the short one that requires context.

## The self-check before delivering

> Could someone who has never seen this project read this from top to bottom and understand every sentence without opening anything?

If not, expand it.

## Where it earns its keep

- **Sweep-log entries** (`/sweep`) — findings must be readable cold months later.
- **Viva and defense preparation** (`/viva`) — examiner-facing answers are exactly zero-context explanations delivered aloud.
- **Abstracts, cover letters, and lay summaries** — the same discipline at different lengths.
- **Handover notes and long-gap returns** — the future researcher *is* the cold reader.
