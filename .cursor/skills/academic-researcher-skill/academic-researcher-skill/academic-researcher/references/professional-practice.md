# Professional Practice — Dissemination, Collaboration, Funding

Reference for `/conference`, `/coauthor`, and `/funding` — the professional scaffolding around the papers. These matter disproportionately for the independent researcher, who lacks the institutional channels a department supplies.

## `/conference` — talks, posters, and conference strategy

Conferences provide early feedback, visibility, and a network — particularly valuable when no department surrounds the candidate. Help the researcher:
- **Select venues** that match the work's stage and field; distinguish prestigious selective conferences from those that merely confer attendance.
- **Write the conference abstract** to the call's criteria, which differ from a journal abstract (often more forward-looking, sometimes blind-reviewed).
- **Convert a paper into a talk** — a talk argues one idea, not the whole paper; structure for a listening (not reading) audience, with the contribution stated early and a clear takeaway.
- **Design a poster** — a poster is a visual conversation-starter, not a paper on a wall: minimal text, one clear message, legible from a distance.
- **Prepare for questions** — anticipate the room's challenges (route to `/viva` for rehearsal); conference Q&A is low-stakes practice for the defense.
- **Sequence relative to publication** — be aware of how presenting interacts with prior-publication and embargo norms in the field.

## `/coauthor` — authorship, contribution, and collaboration

Authorship is an ethical and strategic matter, and a frequent source of dispute. Help the researcher:
- **Apply contribution norms** — the CRediT taxonomy and the field's authorship conventions; substantial intellectual contribution justifies authorship, not funding or seniority alone.
- **Decide authorship order** early and explicitly, by the field's convention (first-author-led, alphabetical, last-author-as-senior), and record the agreement before disputes arise.
- **Manage the supervisor/mentor relationship** where one exists — expectations, feedback cadence, and co-authorship of the candidate's papers, which is normal but should be negotiated.
- **Navigate the thesis requirement** — for a PhD by publication, institutions typically require the candidate to be the principal contributor and to document their specific contribution to each co-authored paper; plan for this from the outset.
- **Handle disagreements** constructively and, where serious, through the appropriate research-integrity channel.

Claude advises on norms and drafts contribution statements; it does not adjudicate disputes or determine who merits authorship — those are the parties' responsibilities, guided by institutional policy.

## `/funding` — grants and fellowships

Independent and self-funded researchers often need to secure support. Help the researcher:
- **Identify suitable schemes** — doctoral fellowships, research grants, small-project and travel funds; match eligibility and remit honestly, using real web search to find current calls rather than asserting programs from memory.
- **Read the call as the assessment criteria** — write to what the funder will score, not to a generic template.
- **Articulate significance and feasibility** — the two axes most funders weigh: why it matters, and why this researcher can deliver it.
- **Structure the proposal** — aims, background and gap, approach, timeline, budget justification, and expected outputs, calibrated to the scheme's length and audience (often non-specialist reviewers).
- **Plan a pipeline** — small, fast wins build the track record that larger awards reward; sequence applications against the program timeline.

Claude scaffolds and sharpens the case; the research vision and its merit remain the researcher's.
## `/network` — building the scholarly network

The independent researcher's sharpest disadvantage is the absence of the network a department supplies: critical readers, mentors, collaborators, and — for the viva systems — a sense of the examiner pool. Help the researcher build it deliberately:
- **Secure critical readers before submission.** At least one knowledgeable human should read each paper and the synthesis chapter before a journal or examiner does; the skill's adversarial commands (`/devils-advocate`, `/committee`) supplement but do not replace a real reader. Help identify who, in the field, might serve.
- **Find mentors and collaborators.** Identify scholars whose work is closest to the candidate's, and structure principled outreach — a specific, concise, respectful approach that offers something, not only asks. Claude drafts such messages; the candidate reviews and sends them (Claude does not send on their behalf).
- **Engage scholarly communities.** Relevant learned societies, special-interest groups, seminar series, preprint communities, and academic networks; conferences (see `/conference`) are the highest-yield channel.
- **Understand the examiner landscape (viva systems).** In the UK/EU/AUS, examiners are typically established scholars in the area; being aware of who the field's authorities are — and engaging with their work honestly — is part of preparing both the thesis and the defense. Selection is the institution's process, not the candidate's, but informed awareness helps.
- **Build peer support.** Other doctoral researchers, writing groups, and accountability partners counter the isolation that derails independent candidates (see `/momentum`).

Claude advises on strategy and drafts outreach; relationships and their conduct remain the researcher's.
